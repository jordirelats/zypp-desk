Getting started

Zypp Desk is a window for zypper, the program that installs, removes and updates software on openSUSE. Everything Zypp Desk does, it does by running zypper, and it always shows you the exact command.

Three ideas make it safe to use:

  • Nothing changes until you apply it. What you choose goes to the queue at the bottom of the window. You can look at it (Review), try it (Simulate) and only then make it real (Apply).
  • Every question says what will happen in plain words, what it means for you, and the exact command underneath. Pressing Enter never starts a change: you have to choose the button that does it.
  • You can always see what zypper says. The terminal at the bottom (Show the terminal, in the sidebar) shows zypper's own output, and the panel next to it explains its warnings: how serious they are and what to do.

Changing the system needs the administrator password. Zypp Desk asks for it when it is needed and the computer remembers it for five minutes, so several steps in a row ask only once.

Start on the Home page: a big search field and cards that tell you whether there are updates, how your repositories are and what needs a look.

The queue: Review, Simulate and Apply

Installing, removing, locking a package, adding a repository or importing a key: each of these is a change, and it waits in the queue until you apply it. The bar at the bottom shows how many there are and the commands they will run.

  • Review lists the changes in the order they will run, in words. Each one has its own button to take it out of the queue. When a change needs another one (a program needs the repository it comes from, and the repository needs its key), the list says so, and Zypp Desk asks before you take out something others need.
  • Simulate asks zypper what it would really do, without changing anything: what else it would install or remove, how much it would download, and whether there is a problem. If there is one, you can choose a solution in plain words.
  • Apply shows the changes once more and runs them. If a step fails, Zypp Desk stops there: what was done stays done, and the rest stays in the queue to try again.
  • Clear empties the queue. It undoes nothing, because nothing has changed yet.

Simulate only tries the package changes: changes to repositories, locks, languages and Flatpak apps have no dry run, and they run only when you apply.

Keeping the system up to date

openSUSE Tumbleweed gets a new version every few days. On the Updates page you find:

  • Your routine: the steps you usually run to update, for example refresh the repositories, download the updates, then install them. Run the routine runs them one after the other and shows each step. Edit the routine… changes it with buttons, or as text, as you would type it in a terminal.
  • What there is to update: packages from the repositories, Flatpak apps, programs from the web and firmware, with a box to tick each one. The card on the right tells you about the package you choose.
  • Simulate the dup…: the full upgrade of Tumbleweed (zypper dup) in its own window, with its options.

A big update

When an update is big, or the new Tumbleweed came out less than a day ago, Zypp Desk asks whether it is worth waiting. It tells you how big it is, how new the version is and whether the computer is on battery. You can update now, only download it now (installing it later is much faster), or be reminded tomorrow: the icon next to the clock will tell you.

Little room on the disk

If the update does not fit on the disk with room to spare, Zypp Desk offers to free space first (packages kept from earlier downloads, old kernels) and to update in parts. Each part is a normal zypper update of some packages, downloaded completely before it is installed: if a download fails, nothing of that part is installed, and the system stays as the previous part left it. A normal upgrade finishes what is left.

Installing and removing programs

  • Type a name in the search field of Home or Packages. Packages can search the descriptions, what a package provides or needs, its files, and the lists of orphaned or unneeded packages.
  • Applications shows the programs you have, wherever they come from (a repository, Flatpak or the web), and the ones with an update. Discover by theme shows the catalogues of openSUSE and Flathub by category.
  • The card of a package has its information, its versions (you can install an older one), what it needs, its files and its changes.
  • Remove… asks whether to also remove what only that program needed. Zypp Desk lists those packages first, so you see what goes.
  • Lock keeps a package as it is: zypper will not update or remove it until you unlock it in Locks.
  • Flatpak has the Flatpak apps, their updates, their remotes and their permissions. They go to the same queue.

If the program is not in your repositories, Search online… looks for it in the openSUSE Build Service, Fedora COPR and Flathub, and explains how much you can trust each source.

Programs from the web and .rpm files

Some programs are published only as files on their web site or on GitHub. In Programs from the web Zypp Desk follows them: it looks for new versions, downloads them, checks them (the signature, or the checksum the author publishes) and puts them in the queue. After they are installed, the files go to the folder you chose, which can be a local zypper repository.

You can also open a downloaded .rpm file with Zypp Desk (from the file manager, Open with Zypp Desk). It tells you who signed it, what it needs and what it would replace, before anything is installed.

Programs made for Fedora

Some .rpm files are made for Fedora and ask for things by their Fedora names. When openSUSE has the same thing under another name, Zypp Desk shows you the list (Fedora name, openSUSE name, why) and can make a small bridge package that tells zypper they are the same. The bridge has no files; it is made on your computer and is listed in Programs from the web, which tells you when it is no longer needed. If something has no openSUSE equivalent, Zypp Desk says which and makes no bridge.

Repositories and signing keys

A repository is a place zypper downloads packages from. In Repositories you can enable or disable them, change their priority (a smaller number wins when the same package is in several), check which ones do not answer, change their address, and ask Zypp Desk to propose an order.

Some programs made for Fedora leave their repository in /etc/yum.repos.d, where zypper does not look, so they would never update. Zypp Desk notices it and offers to add the repository to zypper, with its key.

A signing key proves who made a package or a repository. In Signing keys you see the keys your computer trusts, whose they are and when they expire, and you can import a new one from a web address, a file or its fingerprint, comparing it with the one the vendor publishes. Only trust a key from a source you trust: a key lets its owner install anything on your computer.

Services are lists of repositories kept by someone else (openSUSE keeps the Tumbleweed ones).

When something goes wrong

  • A dependency problem: zypper cannot do what you asked without changing something else. Zypp Desk shows each problem in words with the solutions zypper offers, and recommends the one that changes least. When there are many, it first groups them by cause: often a repository built on top of Tumbleweed (VideoLAN, Packman) has not been rebuilt for the new version yet, and the problems go away by themselves in a day or two. You can wait and be reminded, update the rest and leave those as they are, or look at them one by one.
  • A file is not found while downloading: right after a new Tumbleweed version, the mirrors take a few hours to copy it. Try again in an hour.
  • Something broke after an update: History shows what changed in each update and, when the system makes snapshots (snapper), lets you undo the changes or go back to the system as it was.
  • A warning you do not understand: the panel next to the terminal explains it, says how serious it is and what to do.
  • Maintenance checks broken dependencies, removes old kernels, cleans the cache and tells you which programs still use old files and whether the computer needs a restart.

Notifications and settings

A small icon next to the clock tells you when there are updates. It starts when you log in; you can turn it off, or change how often it looks, in Settings. Zypp Desk installs a timer that refreshes the repositories every six hours, so the count is up to date. It never installs anything by itself. If you turn it on in Settings, it also downloads the next update in advance, only when the computer is plugged in and not on a metered connection (a phone hotspot).

Settings also has the options of zypp.conf (recommended packages, how many kernels to keep, connections per download…), the terminal, and which program opens .rpm files. The folder for programs from the web is changed in Programs from the web (Change the folder…).
